The improvement loop

Learn. Test. Debug. Improve. Suggest a commit. Retest.

Sol.College is a flywheel for how fast a Solana dApp can improve. A lesson or a finding is meant to end in something a repository can take: a patch or pull request against the working practice fork, with a regression test, then a retest on devnet. Verified fixes are pushed to the working fork and opened as pull requests to the original project, for the team to accept or decline.

How it works

How Sol.College works

  1. 01

    Choose your pathway

    Start with dApp development, product testing and QA, or security research. Choose challenges appropriate to your experience and the skills you want to develop.

  2. 02

    Enter the project environment

    Access an isolated fork of a participating Solana application, together with its learning brief, documentation and permitted testing scope.

    Understand what you can test, what you cannot test and what a useful contribution looks like.

  3. 03

    Learn, investigate and test

    Work through guided tasks or explore approved research questions. Inspect the code, follow user journeys, reproduce errors and test your assumptions.

  4. 04

    Submit the evidence

    Turn your work into something another person can evaluate: a test case, reproducible issue, technical explanation, proposed patch or structured product review.

  5. 05

    Improve through feedback

    Use review and triage feedback to refine your reasoning, strengthen your report and understand what happens next.

Frequently asked questions

What do you mean by a live fork?

An interactive, isolated environment based on a participating project’s application or codebase—not simply a screenshot, video or static code example.

The exact components, network, dependencies and available features are specified for each environment. A fork may differ from the production application, and findings may need further validation by the project team.

Do I need to be an experienced Solana developer?

The pathway should match your starting point. Some challenges focus on understanding application flows and testing user journeys; others require programming knowledge or security research experience.

Each challenge states its prerequisites so you can choose work that is appropriate for you.

Am I testing the project’s production application?

Not unless a separate programme explicitly authorises it.

The default learning environment is the provided fork. Permission to use that environment does not extend to production systems, real user accounts or third-party infrastructure.

Will I earn a bug bounty?

Only where a participating programme explicitly offers one and your submission meets its published terms.

Course participation, challenge completion and submission of a potential issue do not automatically qualify for payment.

What happens to a finding?

You submit it through the designated process with the evidence needed to assess it. The project can then determine whether it is reproducible, relevant to its production system, already known or in need of further investigation.

A submission is not a confirmed vulnerability until it has been appropriately validated.

Can I share my work publicly?

Only within the relevant permissions and disclosure rules. Some learning outputs may be suitable for a public portfolio; security findings and confidential project information may need to remain private.

Does this replace a security audit?

No. Educational testing and community research can contribute useful findings, but they are not a guarantee of security or a substitute for appropriately scoped professional review.

How can my project participate?

Start by proposing an application, feature or challenge. Together, we define the environment, learning objectives, testing scope, deliverables and review process before opening it to participants.
